# From chart to action

## First two weeks

**Days 1–2: agree on the definitions.** Confirm the clock that starts an SLA, whether weekends count, which states pause the clock, and how reopened work is counted. These details can move the SLA rate materially.

**Days 3–5: look at late cases, not just summary numbers.** Pull a small sample from technical faults and returns/refunds. Include long cases with repeated handoffs and compare them with similar cases that were resolved more quickly.

**Days 6–8: choose one friction point.** Examples include a required detail on intake, a clear owner for the next step, a direct route for a specific fault, or a limit on transfers without a reason code.

**Days 9–12: test in one queue.** Record start/end dates, staffing, incoming volume, and any other process change. Compare p90 resolution time, SLA attainment, reopen rate and rating with a comparable queue or pre-agreed baseline.

**Days 13–14: decide what to keep.** Keep changes that improve the wait without simply shifting work to another team. If the results are mixed, inspect the cases rather than relying on the summary alone.

## Keep the scorecard balanced

Every operational review should carry at least one measure of time (median and p90), one measure of promise-keeping (SLA), one measure of repeat effort (reopens or handoffs), and one measure of customer experience. Review these together before moving targets or shifting staffing.
